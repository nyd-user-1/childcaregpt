Most families who qualify for child care help in New York never apply. The form is long, the rules read like a tax code, and people self-disqualify based on guesses. This post is the opposite of the form — a short, honest gut-check you can run in five minutes to know if it's worth going further.

The program is the <Term id="ccap">Child Care Assistance Program (CCAP)</Term>. If you'd like the full walk-through, see [Can you get help paying for child care in New York?](/blog/help-paying-for-child-care). This post is just the door.

## The four questions

### 1. Do you have a child under 13?

CCAP covers care for children **under 13** — or up to 19 if the child has a documented disability or is in foster care. If your youngest is 14, this isn't your program.

### 2. Are you working, looking for work, or in school?

CCAP exists to make work or training possible. You qualify if you're:

- Working (any number of hours, including part-time and gig)
- Actively looking for work (limited, but it counts)
- In an approved education or training program
- In treatment that makes care necessary

Both parents in a two-parent household need a qualifying activity. The bar is "doing something," not "earning a lot."

### 3. Is your household income roughly at or below 85% of the <Term id="state-median-income">State Median Income</Term>?

This is the threshold most people trip on — usually because they assume their income is too high.

Rough 2026 numbers for 85% SMI:

- Family of 2 — about $5,700/month
- Family of 3 — about $7,000/month
- Family of 4 — about $8,400/month
- Family of 5 — about $9,700/month

If your household is at or below those numbers, you likely qualify on income. Above them, you may still qualify in some circumstances — many counties have local rules.

> A household of four earning $100,000 is right at the edge in many counties. People with that income often assume "we make too much" and never check. They shouldn't assume.

### 4. Are you a New York State resident?

You need to live in New York. That's it. You don't need to be a U.S. citizen — eligibility is based on the child's status, not the parent's, and many immigration statuses qualify.

## What you'd actually pay

If you qualify, CCAP pays your provider directly. You pay a **family share** — a co-pay calculated from your income, usually a small fraction of the full cost.

A family of three with one infant in Westchester County is looking at roughly $1,300/month in unsubsidized infant center care. With CCAP at the lower-income end of eligibility, the family share might be under $100/month. Even at the upper end, it's often a few hundred — a fraction of the sticker price.

## If three of four are yes, apply

The fourth question that trips people up is almost always income — and the income limit is higher than people think. The realistic rule is: **if you answered yes to questions 1, 2, and 4, apply.** Let the county check the income math. They will tell you for free.

Worst case: you don't qualify and you've spent an hour. Best case: you qualify and shave thousands off your annual child care bill.

To start, take the in-app eligibility check or contact your <Term id="dss">county Department of Social Services</Term>. Counties run CCAP, so the office you deal with is local.

There's no penalty for applying and being denied. The only loss is from never asking.
