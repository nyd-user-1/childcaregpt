Touring child care is its own kind of fatigue. Every place greets you warmly, every room is wiped down for the visit, and the polite half-hour leaves you with a vague impression that "they seemed nice." Three tours in, you can't tell them apart.

Here's the short, honest version of what to look at, what to ask, and what to weigh.

## Before you walk in

Look the provider up first. New York maintains a public license record for every regulated provider — DCC, FDC, GFDC, SACC — that shows the license history and any inspection findings. The provider's own brochure won't mention these. The state's record will.

A few things to note from the record:

- **License status.** Active, conditional, or expired. Stop if it's not active.
- **Inspection findings.** Citations are common and not all are serious. The pattern matters more than any single one. Repeated supervision or ratio violations are a different category than a one-time paperwork miss.
- **How long they've been licensed.** Stability isn't everything, but a provider in their tenth year of operation has earned a different baseline than one in their first six months.

Five minutes of this saves an afternoon.

## What to actually look at on the tour

Polite tours all look the same. Here's what's hard to fake:

**The teachers.** Watch them with the kids for two minutes without saying anything. Are they at the children's eye level? Do they narrate what's happening — "I see you stacking the red one" — or just supervise from across the room? Warmth toward kids who aren't trying to be charming is the tell.

**The transitions.** Anyone can run a calm circle time. Watch how the staff moves a group from one activity to the next, or handles a meltdown, or manages bathroom time. The transitions are where the operation either works or doesn't.

**The walls.** Children's actual artwork — messy, asymmetric, signed by a four-year-old — is a good sign. Identical, teacher-prepped craft projects everywhere is a sign of a place that performs for parents.

**The smell.** Daycares smell like daycares. They shouldn't smell like industrial cleaner *or* like nothing was cleaned. A normal mild background is fine.

**The other parents at pickup.** Try to arrange a tour around pickup if you can. The body language of parents at the door — relaxed and chatting vs. clipped and rushed — tells you more than any brochure.

## What to actually ask

Skip the questions the tour already answers. Ask the ones that reveal how the place runs when things are inconvenient:

- **"What happens when my child has a hard morning at drop-off?"** You're listening for a specific protocol, not "oh, we just give them lots of love."
- **"How do you handle staff turnover?"** Every provider has it. Hearing how they think about it tells you whether they treat it as a problem to solve or a fact they live with.
- **"What's your sick policy?"** You want to know exactly when you'll be told to come pick your child up. Vague answers here become hard phone calls later.
- **"How do you handle a behavior issue between two kids?"** You're looking for "we get down to their level and talk it through," not "we separate them" with no further detail.
- **"Can I see the ratio in the room my child would be in, right now?"** You're not auditing. You're just confirming the marketing matches the room.

## The two questions you ask yourself afterward

Walking out, two gut checks:

**Did the kids look at the teachers?** In a healthy room, kids glance at their caregiver constantly — checking in, looking for reassurance, bringing them things. In a room that's just "watched," they don't.

**Could I leave my child here without it ruining my morning?** Not "is this the best place in the county." Just: could I walk out the door at 8 AM and get to work without a knot in my chest. That answer is allowed to override the spreadsheet.

## A note on tour fatigue

If you can, don't do more than two tours in a day. By the third, your brain blurs them into composite memories and you'll lose the small distinctions that actually matter. Take a photo of each entrance and one note immediately after — one specific thing the place did well and one thing that gave you pause. Future-you, picking between three places a week later, will need that.

The license check tells you what's legal. The tour tells you what's loving. You need both.
